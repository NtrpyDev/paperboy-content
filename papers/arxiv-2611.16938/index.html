Key idea

For any finite group, you can pick subsets whose sizes multiply to about the size of the group, and their products still reach every element.

The question

How small can the pieces be and still cover the group?

A group lets you multiply elements, so two subsets \(A\) and \(B\) give a product set \(AB\) of every \(ab\). That product has at most \(|A|\cdot|B|\) elements, so to reach all of \(G\) the sizes must multiply to at least \(|G|\). Rohrbach's conjecture asks whether that counting limit can nearly be met.

What they did

A theorem for every finite group, with any split of sizes.

The paper proves a statement for every finite group \(G\), every positive integer \(h\), and any nonnegative shares \(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_h\) that add up to 1. The abstract does not describe the method of proof.

What they found

Small subsets multiply out to the whole group.

There are subsets \(A_1, \ldots, A_h\) with \(G = A_1 \cdots A_h\): each set is at most \(|G|\) raised to its share \(\alpha_i\), and every set after the first may be bigger by one absolute constant \(C\). Taking equal shares gives a single set \(A\) with \(A^h = G\) and size at most \(c_h\) times the h-th root of \(|G|\).

Why it matters

An open conjecture, settled for finite groups.

If the proof holds, every finite group has a small set whose h-fold product is the whole group, within a constant of the smallest size counting allows.
