Key idea

Decades of oxygen and CO2 records in the air show the ocean sink growing while the net land sink did not.

The question

Half our carbon disappears, but where, and is that changing?

The ocean and the plants and soils on land absorb roughly half of the carbon dioxide we release by burning fossil fuels. Each is a carbon sink. How these sinks have changed over time is hard to pin down, because measurements are sparse.

What they did

They read the air, both its oxygen and its CO2.

The team used 30+ years of measurements of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. From these records they estimate the ocean sink and the net land sink, and how each changed.

What they found

The ocean sink grew, the land sink held flat.

The ocean sink averaged 2.8 ± 0.4 petagrams of carbon a year and rose by 0.63 ± 0.19 each decade. The net land sink averaged 1.0 ± 0.5 and shows no robust trend, despite large swings from decade to decade.

Why it matters

Climate forecasts lean on these models, and the air disagrees with many.

The results challenge many process-based simulations of the land and ocean sinks, which questions how well those models can predict future climate.
