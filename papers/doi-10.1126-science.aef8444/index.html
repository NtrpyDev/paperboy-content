Key idea

Some undersea landslides are not one sudden collapse but a slow, drawn-out retreat up the slope.

The question

Undersea landslides had never been watched, only inferred.

Where rivers build deltas into the sea, the sandy slopes beneath the water can fail and slide into deep water. These fully submarine landslides threaten seabed structures and carry sediment, carbon, and pollutants to the deep sea. None had been monitored directly in nature, so ideas about how they fail, including slow breaching in sand, were untested there.

What they did

They watched a delta slope from under the water.

The team monitored fully submarine landslides directly, underwater, at the Homathko Delta in western Canada.

What they found

The failures crept uphill and kept the current running.

The landslides retreated slowly upslope, the signature of breaching. Because they last, they start sustained turbidity currents that carry sediment, carbon, and pollutants farther offshore.

Why it matters

Seabed structures may face a long push, not a single blow.

The authors say the prolonged nature of these failures matters for the stress seabed structures must be designed to withstand, and for how far sediment, carbon, and pollutants travel offshore.
