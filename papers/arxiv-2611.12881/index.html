Key idea

Whether a companion belongs with the planets or with the stars seems to depend on its weight relative to its star, not on one fixed mass.

The question

A gap between planets and stars. Where does it sit?

Many stars have a companion: a giant planet, a brown dwarf, or another star. Close in, brown dwarfs are scarce. Surveys find them around fewer than 1% of Sun-like stars within about 5 au. Astronomers call this the brown dwarf desert.

Textbooks draw the brown dwarf line at a fixed mass, about 13 Jupiter masses, where a body can briefly burn deuterium. That is a rule about the body's insides, not about how it formed. The other candidate is the mass ratio: the companion's weight as a share of its star's. The two ideas only part ways when you compare stars of different sizes.

Earlier hints from other methods put a desert at mass ratios of about 2% to 5%. But each method saw only a narrow slice of stars and orbits. No single survey had covered planets and stellar companions together across many sizes of star.

What they did

A wobble on the sky, measured 25 years apart.

A companion and its star circle a shared balance point, so the star itself sways on the sky. Astrometry measures that sway. The Hipparcos satellite charted star motions, and Gaia charted them again about 25 years later. If a star's motion in each snapshot disagrees with its average motion in between, something unseen is pulling on it.

The team began with 96,915 bright stars within 1 kpc and kept the 63,254 (65%) that have not evolved beyond the red-giant branch. Their masses run from 0.16 to 8.1 times the Sun's. Most stars' signals are faint and ambiguous on their own, so the team did not try to list detections.

What they found

The gap keeps one ratio. Its mass slides with the star.

The team sorted stars by mass into six groups, from small M stars to massive B stars, and fit each group on its own. In every group from K to B, the floor landed near the same mass ratio, about 2%, and one constant ratio fits all five groups well.†

Caveat

† The M group had only 119 stars and was left out of this fit. The paper sees hints that the trend continues there.

The desert is deep, not a wiggle. At the floor, companions are about 33 times rarer than at the planet-side peak and about 19 times rarer than at the star-side peak.

Because the floor follows the ratio, one mass can land on either side. For a Sun-like star, a 1% ratio is about 10 Jupiter masses, and in the paper's G-star group the floor sits near 17 Jupiter masses. Around K stars, the lightest star-like companions weigh about 13. Around B stars, the heaviest planet-like companions weigh about 27, well past the deuterium line.

Metal content tells the two sides apart too. Planets that grow from a rocky core should be more common around stars rich in heavy elements, a property called metallicity. In 18,672 F, G and K stars, roughly Sun-like in size, companions below the desert grow more common as metallicity rises, and companions above it grow less common.

That split fits two recipes: planets built up from dust and rock, and stellar companions made when gas breaks into pieces. The paper calls this plausible. It sees the gap and the opposite trends, not formation itself.

  • Floor1.76%With a range of −0.19 to +0.26 points. One ratio fits the five group minima well (χ² of 1.6 for 4 degrees of freedom).
  • Depth33×Below the planet peak, even though the model's starting assumption leaned toward no desert at all.
  • ChecksIt recovers known binaries, cold Jupiters and brown dwarfs, agrees with radial-velocity and imaging surveys on the same stars, and shows no color or sky-position bias.
  • Limits
  • ReviewPreprintNot peer reviewed. It relies on a companion catalog paper that is also unpublished. The per-star results can be browsed online, but the formal data release, code and population results are promised only on acceptance.
  • Orbits3–94yearsAbout 2 to 20 au only. Beyond about 100 years the method cannot separate a companion's mass from its distance.
  • M stars119starsToo few to include in the ratio fit, though the abstract says the result spans M to B stars.
  • InferredMasses come from models, and most single stars' signals are weak. The rates describe the fitted population, not a list of found objects.
  • Metals2.6σThe falling trend above the desert is weaker evidence than the rising one below it (4.4σ), and covers F, G and K stars only.
  • DisagreementAnother survey (Cui et al.) finds companion rates several times higher: 23.1% against 3.0% here, for 5 to 120 Jupiter-mass companions of similar stars. The authors suggest that survey's completeness correction may explain it; this is not settled.

  • Wider orbitsDoes the ratio desert hold beyond about 100 years, and for M stars?

Why it matters

Planet or not? Ask about the star first.

If the result holds, a fixed mass line such as 13 Jupiter masses is the wrong way to split planets from star-like companions on these orbits. A body of 27 Jupiter masses around a B star can belong with the planets, while a lighter one around a K star belongs with the stars.

It also shows a new way to take a census: thousands of faint wobbles, none convincing alone, pooled into one population. The per-star results can already be browsed online; the formal data release and the code are promised once the paper is accepted.
