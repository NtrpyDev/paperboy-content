Mammalian cells that swallowed whole algal cells sometimes ended up with algal DNA inside their nuclei, and hybrid cell lines held on to chromosome-scale pieces of it. Key idea

DNA methylation looks like the gatekeeper that lets a mammalian nucleus settle a distant eukaryote's chromosomes rather than lose them.

The question

Gene transfer between species is a bacterial story, and eukaryotes were the gap in it.

Horizontal gene transfer drives fast evolutionary innovation, but the well-substantiated cases are largely confined to prokaryotes, the single-celled life without a nucleus. Transfer from one eukaryote to another stays exceptionally rare, and the abstract says direct demonstrations at the scale of a whole genome are absent. So: can a mammalian cell take in another eukaryote's DNA by the genome, and hold it?

What they did

They borrowed the trick that made chloroplasts, then watched the DNA arrive.

Inspired by endosymbiotic genome acquisition, the team had mammalian cells engulf intact algal cells, and compared that route with cell fusion. Live-cell imaging caught the transferred algal DNA appearing early in the nucleus after fusion, while genome sequencing and cytogenetic analyses tracked what was still there later. They also tested cells made deficient in a de novo DNA methyltransferase, the enzyme that places new methyl marks on DNA.

What they found

Algal chromosomes stayed, wrapped in silencing marks.

Hybrid cells maintained more than 16 Mb of algal DNA, including intact chromosomes, as telomere-linked structures that persisted for 95 generations without selection. The transferred DNA gained methylation progressively and picked up repressive histone modifications, forming heterochromatic domains that physically associate with host chromosomes. Cells deficient in the de novo DNA methyltransferase established and retained the algal DNA less well, which the authors read as a gatekeeping role for methylation.

Why it matters

A big piece of foreign DNA can be housed, if the host silences it.

The findings point to an epigenetic mechanism that helps a cell accommodate large-scale foreign DNA from a distantly related eukaryote. That gives a handle on how such transfers could ever stick, and on what limits them.
