Key idea

Under the paper's stated assumptions, there is only one kind of steady, spinning black hole in empty space: Kerr's.

The question

Is Kerr the only rotating black hole, or just the one we know?

In general relativity, the Kerr solution describes a black hole that spins at a steady rate in empty space. The open question is whether every such black hole looks like Kerr from outside, in its domain of outer communications, without assuming the spacetime is analytic or close to Kerr to begin with.

What they did

They gave the hole a charge on paper, then took a limit.

The authors apply a Harrison transform to build a family of solutions of the Einstein–Maxwell equations. Comparing areas of null surfaces with the area-charge inequality, they pin down the limiting transformed geometry.

What they found

The limit has exact identities, and they lead to Kerr.

With the mass normalized to \(M = 1\), the bifurcate sphere of the limiting geometry has area radius one and constant electric and magnetic flux densities. Those identities meet the technical assumptions of a theorem by Ionescu and Klainerman, which then identifies the spacetime with subextremal Kerr.
