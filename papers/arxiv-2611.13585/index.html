Picture a mail carrier who must pass every house and return, in a town where every street counts as one block. Finding the shortest route is hard, so researchers prove guarantees instead. A new algorithm’s route is never longer than 89/64, about 1.3906, times a lower bound on the best route. The previous guarantee, 7/5, had stood for 14 years.

Key idea

Let the algorithm delete a whole bundle of roads at a junction, and charge it a fixed 2 when it deletes too much. That small freedom moves the guarantee from 7/5 to 89/64.

The question

Visit every vertex and come home, on as few edges as possible.

A graph is a set of points (vertices) joined by lines (edges), like towns and roads. In graphic TSP every road counts as one step. A tour must visit every town and return to the start, and it may walk a road twice. Its length is the number of roads walked.

The paper sees a tour another way: copies of roads that keep everything connected and give every town an even number of road ends. Then one closed walk uses each copy exactly once.

Deleting roads is cheaper than doubling them, but delete the wrong ones and the town falls apart. Choosing what to delete is the hard part. The problem is NP-hard: no fast method is known that always finds the shortest tour.

So researchers prove an approximation ratio for a fast method. The yardstick is a lower bound that every tour must meet, the subtour LP. Christofides reached 3/2 in 1976. Later work brought graphic TSP down to 7/5, and there it stuck. Goemans conjectured that the true limit of this yardstick is 4/3.

What they did

Cut whole bundles of edges. Pay 2 when that goes too far.

The algorithm, StarMatch, starts from the lower bound’s solution and explores the graph depth first. Then it picks which edges to delete and which to double, in one optimization a computer can solve exactly in polynomial time through b-matching.

Step 1 of 4

Explore the graph depth first from a root. The straight tree edges reach every vertex. Each curve is a back edge, climbing back up to an ancestor.

Step 2 of 4

Group each tree edge with every back edge that climbs from below it to its top vertex. Each group is a removable star. This graph has three. The faint edges belong to no star and always stay.

Step 3 of 4

Delete all but one edge from every star at once. What is left still reaches every vertex, as a spanning tree. The earlier method of Mömke and Svensson could delete only one edge of each prescribed pair.

Step 4 of 4

The new optimization may even delete a whole star. Then the graph splits, so the algorithm adds back two copies of one star edge, for a fixed cost of 2.

That fixed price is the trick. It turns a cost that is not linear into one the optimization can plan around.

To prove the guarantee, nobody runs the algorithm on every graph. The authors build one fractional solution the optimization could have chosen and add up its cost. The true optimum can only be cheaper.

Raising the tree edge of each star lowers this fractional solution’s cost, and that cost caps what the optimization finds. There is a catch: raising one star can add cost at the star below it, so the paper picks each dS with care. The proof then blends two such solutions, \( Y = \tfrac34\, Q^{(1)} + \tfrac14\, Q^{(2)} \), and counts the cost star by star.

What they found

Below 7/5, by 3/320.

The gain is 3/320, or 0.009375: a relative gain of about 0.67 percent, by our arithmetic from 7/5 and 89/64. It is the first improvement on 7/5 in 14 years, by the authors’ count. It also proves the integrality gap of the lower bound is at most 89/64 on graphic instances.

The paper runs no experiments. It does work one example by hand: a graph with 23 vertices and 30 edges.

StarMatch lands one edge above the lower bound: 27 against 26 (27/26, about 1.038, by our arithmetic). One hand-picked example shows the method can win, not by how much it usually does.

On graphs where every vertex has many independent connections, the guarantee drops further.†

Caveat

† The method also gives 4/3 on 3-connected graphs with maximum degree at most 4. The paper notes that a variant of Newman’s earlier argument already gives that, so it is not shown as new.

Why it matters

A stuck number moves again.

Graphic TSP is a proving ground: methods that work here get tried on the general problem, where roads have different lengths. This result adds a simple tool, removable stars with a fixed price to reconnect, and shows that 7/5 was not a wall.

The conjectured 4/3 still sits below the new 89/64, so the gap between proof and conjecture is not closed. The authors report further work, checked in the Lean proof assistant, that reaches 14524/10481, about 1.38575, but this paper only sketches those proofs.
